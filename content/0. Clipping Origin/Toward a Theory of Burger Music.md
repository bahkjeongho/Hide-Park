The most inescapable phrase in the online music underground of late is “burger music.” “Bro, Che is burger music,” the TikTok nanoinfluencer idkzxop [said](https://www.tiktok.com/@idkzxop/video/7636136204160814366) this week. “I’m not gonna elaborate. Because if you’re not a burger, you understand.” “This is just millennial burger music, fire video tho,” an X user [wrote](https://x.com/panchoisbored/status/2051064992166810113) about Yung Lean’s recent campfire catharsis tune “STORM.” But what does “burger music” even entail? Is it Instagram Reels rap for people with no taste? A synonym for “coworker music,” itself a convoluted term that usually refers to bad pop songs your blandest colleague would fall for? For now, it seems to mean so many things to so many people that it ends up meaning nothing at all.

There’s no exact origin for the phrase. “Burger” and its sibling “ham”(burger) were big insults in [Detroit](https://www.reddit.com/r/CrimeInTheD/comments/1en71b8/favorite_detroit_slang_words/) around the turn of the decade; BabyTron has on multiple occasions said, “Yo boyfriend a burger.” The newest iteration appears to have been popularized by Twitch streamers [Prince Cassell](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DSZEeYxEXPM/) and [Macc Danny](https://www.tiktok.com/@macdannyyy/video/7607962188510235934), who use “burger” as a synonym for a musician who’s goofy but acts like they’re doing something impressive or edgy. Their earliest and biggest target has been [Dax](https://www.tiktok.com/@macdannyyy/video/7606859216212495647), a 32-year-old janitor-turned-rapper from Canada who spits overwrought motivational lyrics over rudimentary beats.

Over the last few months, “burger music” has expanded to encompass more unexpectedly creative styles. Maybe the biggest recipient of vitriol is an 18-year-old named [Slayr](https://pitchfork.com/artists/slayr/), whose real last name, thanks to cosmic fate, is McDonald. Detractors portray him as a [pixelated cartoon character](https://www.tiktok.com/@swagdem.de/video/7628325872612724000), his torso replaced by a gleaming slab of meat. One tweet features an [AI cover](https://x.com/yohellohiwsp/status/2047733665363349623/photo/1) of a fake Slayr album called *BURGERMAN* that imagines him as a half-burger, half-human hybrid engulfed in a hellish flood of grease and cheese splatter. When I type “burger” on Spotify, Slayr’s profile pops up first (likely due to the many user-generated playlists titled “BURGER MUSIC” stuffed with his songs).

I’ve observed two “burger music” camps. The first camp thinks Slayr is just corny, contrasting the aggro music with his lowkey presentation and [love of anime](https://x.com/countdragons/status/2020649337962041501). These people believe Slayr has “ [negative aura](https://x.com/ItzBasin/status/2009840131763539992) ”; streamer NickAtNite calls his [aesthetic](https://x.com/inmypoloshirt/status/2009849930098319591) “GameStop worker.” Slayr’s ability to sculpt transitions between songs and puke out chord progressions in seconds, to [the astonishment](https://x.com/PlaqueReport/status/2035889736816926871) of PlaqueBoyMax, feeds into a sense that his music is for tryhards.

“Burger” used as an antonym for aura feels like an attempt to reassert the bounds of *cool* after it’s been so destabilized. “Cool” once had specific referents, enshrined in the collective consciousness through TV show, movie, and celebrity tropes. Jocks and mean girls relished in the mainstream, while the freaks and losers huddled over video-game music and alt weirdness. Obviously, this was never fully true, but over the last decade it’s become a lot easier to capitalize on less glamorous hobbies and make nerdiness your brand. SoundCloud rappers were weebs; DnD geeks became huge influencers. The sense of a cultural in- and out-group dissolved as niche communities grew and old-school ideas of coolness fell away. In some sense, the indiscriminate labeling of anything approaching cringe as “burger music” is an effort to laugh certain persona types into oblivion while elevating “real” music or whatever.

Yet there’s nothing immediately cringe about Slayr’s rage rap. He croons about heartbreak and Hellcats over synths that firehose sugar. The downside is it’s tragically consistent. Every song hits with the same indistinguishable gush of pleasant pixels. I’ve found myself reaching for Slayr on auto-pilot sometimes like a dependable standard, but it rarely stirs me into a state of Michelin star hype.

The second, more intriguing camp uses “burger music” to call into question someone’s integrity or investment in the genre. Sometimes, this isn’t the fault of the musicians, it’s their fans’ behavior. Guys like [Luca Guerini](https://x.com/luguerini_/status/2042674880798192015) of the NFR hip-hop podcast and the massive streamer [xQc](https://x.com/bleood/status/2050292099279376625) say they don’t like abrasive rage except for Slayr’s babyfaced harmonies, which offer a clean-shaven spin on the anarchic noise arms race of Che and OsamaSon. This tension is exacerbated by [rating sites](https://rateyourmusic.com/release/mixtape/slayr/half-blood/) propelling Slayr over scene innovators, and creators [declaring him](https://x.com/forthespeakers/status/2048827772118680018) the Kendrick Lamar of his generation, saying his music represents a “ [rebirth of intellectualism](https://www.instagram.com/reels/DXH8gtLCX-w/) ” in hip-hop.

In this sense, the burger music insult springs from a gut sense that the artist’s success depends on listeners who’d scoff at the more experimental musicians who paved the way and haven’t spent time or effort learning about the scene’s origins. It’s easy to see how this could upset in-the-know fans who are already pissed that their favorite music is called brainless or unlistenable.

What strikes me most is not any one definition of burger music but the [relentless discourse](https://www.tiktok.com/@aie6704/video/7615292358216781086) around it. No one consigned to burgerdom faces any real repercussions; in fact it can help. Thanks in part to the memes, Slayr is bigger than ever. The phrase’s opacity is a feature, not a bug; it’s clipfarmed nonsense, the result of content creators witnessing it get popular and using it themselves without understanding what it means beyond the vague circlejerk of mockery. It went viral because of the collision of recognizability (everyone knows what a burger is) with a baffling emptiness, which doubles as a defense to [preempt critique](https://www.tiktok.com/@idkzxop/video/7636136204160814366): *If you’re mad, you’re a burger*. It’s a semantic free-for-all whose surrealism offers a momentary giggle, purpose-built to spark debates about who is or isn’t a real grass-fed genuine burger.

People speak in both the most hyperspecific and vaguest terms, screaming past each other just to get a viral tweet off. The frustrating irony is that there are many real “burgers” worthy of derision—the astroturfed underground artists; the thoughtless thought-leaders lambasting all new rap as rageslop; the platforms incentivizing this bad-faith behavior. But the internet does not reward sanity. The best way to grab attention is to rile people up, and so “burger music” is like a lab-generated buzzword, readymade for maximum outrage and minimum meaning.
