Television has defined 21st century popular culture. And with the rise of Peak TV and streaming, and the flood of film stars to the small screen, an ambitious and diverse new wave of storytellers have revolutionized the medium over the past two-plus decades.

To identify the best work of that period (as we did with [books](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2024/books/best-books-21st-century.html) and [movies](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/movies/best-movies-21st-century.html)), we polled hundreds of stars, showrunners, craftspeople, executives and other industry experts and asked them to vote for the 10 best series that have premiered since Jan. 1, 2000. (So don't expect to see “The Sopranos,” which debuted in 1999.)

Among the more than 500 people who responded were stars like Elisabeth Moss, Adam Scott, Bryan Cranston, Rose Byrne, Colman Domingo and Jason Bateman, and showrunners like Damon Lindelof, Lena Dunham, Lena Waithe and Noah Hawley. See how your favorite shows ranked and save the ones you’ve always meant to watch.

## Scandal

2012–2018

“It’s handled.” When uttered by Olivia Pope, the ultimate D.C. fixer, the “it” might refer to a multinational conspiracy or some minor bit of presidential intrigue. But it had definitely been handled. As the glamorous, steely-eyed Pope, Kerry Washington was the first Black woman to lead a network drama in almost 40 years, and the show turned her into a household name. Fast-paced, sumptuously stylish and full of interracial intimacy — Pope was the long-term lover of the married president of the United States, played by Tony Goldwyn — “Scandal” established the creator Shonda Rhimes, then known for “Grey’s Anatomy,” as a bona fide TV hit-maker with a distinct aesthetic and a feminist point of view. — Salamishah Tillet

## Gilmore Girls

2000–2007

![In a still from “Gilmore Girls,” Alexis Bledel, left, wears a grey sweater and smiles with her arms crossed. Lauren Graham, right, wears a leather jacket and a graphic tee and smiles at Bledel. Trees are in the background.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/20/multimedia/cul-100besttv-gilmore-pvgj/cul-100besttv-gilmore-pvgj-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?width=600&quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

Celebrated shows often push viewers out of their comfort zones. “Gilmore Girls,” created by Amy Sherman-Palladino, succeeded by doing the opposite: charming viewers with low-stakes drama, snappy dialogue and unmatched cozy vibes. The “girls,” Lorelai Gilmore (Lauren Graham) and her daughter, Rory (Alexis Bledel), live in Stars Hollow, Conn., a storybook hamlet teeming with nosy neighbors and quirky townies. Years after its seven-season run ended, “Gilmore Girls” found new life on streaming, becoming synonymous with autumn nostalgia and sending fans to Washington, Conn., the real-life inspiration for Stars Hollow, for a taste of the show’s small-town fantasy. — Maya Salam

## The OA

2016–2019

![In a still from “The OA,” Brit Marling stands behind a dirty glass pane, arms outstretched toward the camera. Her eyes are closed and she is wearing a loose white dress with a grey pattern.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/20/multimedia/cul-100besttv-oa-cpbf/cul-100besttv-oa-cpbf-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?width=600&quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

This memory mystery, about an abductee (Brit Marling) who returns home, forms a support cult, calls herself “The OA” and ardently believes she’s meant for divinity (and revenge), joins “Happy Endings” and “Enlightened” down in Canceled Before Its Time Valley. Marling and Zal Batmanglij came up with this show, which arrived in the thick of our captive white-lady crisis (“Orange Is the New Black,” “Room,” “Unbreakable Kimmy Schmidt,” “Search Party”). The kitchen-sink plot was ludicrous, but “The OA” filled it with nicely dented characters, committed to its exploration of abuse and wound up grazing the sublime. (That cafeteria dance sequence alone...) When Netflix pulled the plug after two seasons, the show clearly had more time to warp and mind to lose. — Wesley Morris

## Community

2009–2015

“Community” once devoted an entire episode to a spoof of “My Dinner With Andre.” On a Thursday night on NBC. This and other improbable prime-time meta stunts — the Ken Burns-style blanket-fort documentary, the stop-motion Christmas special — made Dan Harmon’s absurdist sitcom about a community college study group Must See TV for comedy nerds of all degrees. But it was the committed, emotionally textured performances from Joel McHale, Donald Glover, Gillian Jacobs and the rest of the cast — yes, even Chevy Chase — that kept the show from disappearing up its own rabbit hole. — Jeremy Egner

## Halt and Catch Fire

2014–2017

![In a still from “Halt and Catch Fire,” Mackenzie Davis is seated in a room, wearing an embroidered black jacket and a crossbody bag. She looks away from the camera.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/20/multimedia/cul-100besttv-halt-jmfl/cul-100besttv-halt-jmfl-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?width=600&quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

This drama began in the early days of the personal computer revolution — before social media, before FOMO, before A.I. angst — to tell the story of a quartet of tech pioneers who looked at those clunky beige desktop boxes and saw a kaleidoscopic future. Computers were “the thing that gets us to the thing,” the latter thing being connection, empowerment, even a new kind of art. What began as a familiar tale of business-suite intrigue — “Mad Men” with processors — became a rich, heartfelt, human exploration of the costs and rewards of work and the necessity of failure. Its portrait of ’80s and ’90s techno-optimism may seem quaint now, but its rendering of the love of creation remains bleeding-edge. — James Poniewozik

## House

2004–2012

![In a still from “House M.D.,” Hugh Laurie, dressed in a suit, injects a needle into a patient, who is lying in a hospital bed. Medical equipment is in the background.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/20/multimedia/cul-100besttv-house-jqtf/cul-100besttv-house-jqtf-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?width=600&quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

Network television wasn’t lacking in hospital procedurals when the gruff, pill-popping diagnostician Dr. Gregory House limped onto screens, a character Hugh Laurie owned like he was gobbling the Vicodin himself. But Dr. House had little in common with TV’s usual noble medics, who even when flawed still cared about their patients. Inspired by Sherlock Holmes, Dr. House was intellectual, cold, interested only if he found a case interesting. “House” made it hard to love House, stubbornly eschewing easy sentiment toward or from its lead protagonist. That made the love, when it did come, feel especially earned. — Austin Considine

## The Diplomat

2023–present

A hard-charging diplomat suddenly conscripted to be America’s ambassador to the United Kingdom — and possibly the next U.S. vice president — Kate Wyler (a strenuously de-glammed Keri Russell) seems about as familiar with political niceties as she is with shampoo, which is to say, hardly at all. But it turns out Kate is pretty great at statecraft, and the top-shelf cast (including Rufus Sewell as Kate’s smooth-operator husband and Allison Janney and Bradley Whitford as a combative First Couple) rounds out a crackling drama whose bravura mix of international and personal crises feels like smart-people snack food, in the best way. — Leah Greenblatt

## Station Eleven

![In a still from “Station Eleven,” Himesh Patel is lying on his stomach on the snowy ground, propped up on his arms. I could say it’s unexpected that one of the most uplifting shows of the past quarter-century is set in a postapocalypse, but who would I be kidding? It’s precisely in times of cataclysm and collapse that stories of other people going through it speak loudest. This limited-series adaption of the 2014 Emily St. John Mandel novel, set mostly in the years after a pandemic wiped out most of the world’s population, follows a band of survivors in a Shakespeare troupe traveling and performing in the Lake Michigan area. Wistful, whimsical and stubbornly hopeful, it embodies the idea that making art is the best thing that humans do — and maybe our best shot at outsmarting death. — James Poniewozik

## The Night Of

2016

OK, so the ending felt shrugged. But most of this murder-mystery-courtroom-drama-character-study-prison-show served atmosphere, intrigue, moral indictment and New York City granularity that pooled into American tragedy. Sharply written characters, by Richard Price and Steven Zaillian, receive deeply etched performances from John Turturro, Michael K. Williams and Jeannie Berlin, for starters. Riz Ahmed, who played Naz, a Pakistani American kid accused of killing a white woman, won an Emmy and applied pretzel logic to a portrait of innocence: made it hard, salty and knotted up. — Wesley Morris

## Eastbound & Down

2009–2013

![In a still from “Eastbound & Down,” Danny McBride looks serious as he stands in the foreground of a big room, wearing a yellow shirt and a baseball glove. Kenny Powers: Great American or greatest American? In his HBO comedy, created with Ben Best and Jody Hill, Danny McBride sketched one of TV’s most memorable jackasses in the form of this washed-up ex-Major League pitcher, a colossus of mouthy, mulleted overconfidence who burned out in the midst of his success. A fizzing 40-ounce bottle of pure hubris, McBride’s portrait of unrepentant obnoxiousness knocks it out of the park. — James Poniewozik

## Baby Reindeer

2024

Hilarious and heartbreaking, this Netflix limited series stars Richard Gadd as a struggling London comedian and Jessica Gunning as the ebullient, unhinged stalker who makes his life hell — when she isn’t filling an unnameable void in his soul. Created by Gadd, “Baby Reindeer” followed the path of “Fleabag,” beginning as a solo stage show at Edinburgh Festival Fringe. Like its predecessor, it is a singular stunner, a nervy, mercurial mix of laugh-out-loud comedy and gut-punch despair. — Jeremy Egner

## Dexter

2006–2013

Grouping Dexter Morgan (Michael C. Hall) with the great TV anti-heroes of his age never felt quite right. He was, after all, a genuine serial killer; even at their worst, Tony Soprano and Walter White never assembled kill rooms or organized samples of their victims’ blood. But Dexter had a code: He was a police forensics investigator who killed only violent criminals who had escaped justice. And wouldn’t you know it? Most of the time, you wound up rooting for him, despite having to reconcile that with some of the creepiest, most gruesome scenes in TV history. — Austin Considine

## Reservation Dogs

2021–2023

![In a still from “Reservation Dogs,” Paulina Alexis, Devery Jacobs, D’Pharaoh Woon-A-Tai and Lane Factor walk alongside one another on a street, all wearing dark suits.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/08/24/multimedia/cul-100besttv-reservation-dogs-bkvq/cul-100besttv-reservation-dogs-bkvq-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?width=600&quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

Set among the residents of the Muscogee Nation in rural Oklahoma, this comic drama from Sterlin Harjo and Taika Waititi was a watershed in Indigenous representation. After a frenetic pilot, it relaxed into a looser series, at once hypernaturalistic and surreal. Anchored by the performances of D’Pharaoh Woon-A-Tai, Devery Jacobs, Paulina Alexis and Lane Factor, as the teenage pals at the show’s heart, it dealt both lightly and deeply with themes of loss, growth and belonging. — Alexis Soloski
