In many art circles, fashion photography doesn’t always get the respect it deserves. Plastered across billboards and printed in magazines, these images — of moisturized and windswept models, cradling $5,000 handbags on the beach, by the pool or atop a tenement building — are plainly commercial. Although they exist to sell you something, they can be insidious (and inescapable) reminders that a certain level of wealth or perfection will always be out of reach. “Fashion,” the American photographer [Richard Avedon](https://www.nytimes.com/2004/10/02/obituaries/richard-avedon-the-eye-of-fashion-dies-at-81.html) once said, “is about who you want to be, not who you are.” But despite their limitations, print editorials and brand campaigns are, for many, a portal into a new world, proposing fresh ideas about identity and beauty. They can also provoke: In 1992, a United Colors of Benetton ad [featured a photo](https://www.icp.org/browse/archive/objects/united-colors-of-benetton-aids-david-kirby), originally published in a 1990 issue of Life magazine, of the AIDS activist David Kirby on his deathbed; in 2010, after Italian Vogue printed an oil-spill-themed fashion story shot by Steven Meisel, [The Guardian would ask](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2010/aug/09/vogue-italia-oil-spill-fashion-shoot) if the spread was “slick or crude.”

Choosing the 25 most influential fashion photographs of the past century is an impossible task. Partly, that’s because influence is hard to trace. Without knowing firsthand, one can’t really say whether Marc Jacobs’s 1993 grunge collection (the one that got him fired from Perry Ellis) was inspired by the work of [Joseph Szabo](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/01/22/nyregion/joseph-szabos-teen-spirit-at-the-heckscher-museum-review.html), whose photographs in the 1970s captured the angst and glory of suburban adolescence; or whether today’s street-style photographers admire or even know about the midcentury American photographer [Saul Leiter](https://www.nytimes.com/2013/11/28/arts/saul-leiter-photographer-with-a-palette-for-new-york-dies-at-89.html). There’s also the fact that some fine-art photographers have had a great deal of influence on fashion, even though they aren’t considered fashion photographers and wouldn’t refer to themselves as such. In general, fashion photography was defined broadly enough here to include the work of fine artists, street-style photographers, backstage and nightlife photographers and paparazzi. And it didn’t matter if an image was *meant* as a fashion photograph, so long as the picture later inspired a designer, collection or trend, or it changed how we might think about style.

![A group of people are gathered in an office space with a red wall and gray carpet. Linda Evangelista, Inez van Lamsweerde and Ian Bradley are seated, and Vinoodh Matadin, Cate Holstein, Patrick Li and Nick Haramis are standing by a table.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_12_01/08tmag-fashionimages_12_01-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

The panel, from left: Linda Evangelista, Vinoodh Matadin, Cate Holstein, Inez van Lamsweerde, Patrick Li, Nick Haramis (the conversation’s moderator) and Ian Bradley, photographed at The New York Times on July 14, 2026. Credit... Davey Adésida

One morning this past July, a group of experts convened at the Times building in Midtown Manhattan to debate and make the list below. Huddled in a conference room for nearly three hours, six image makers — the New York-based stylist and T contributor Ian Bradley; the Canadian model Linda Evangelista, one of the original “supers” in the late 1980s and ’90s; the American fashion designer and Khaite founder, Cate Holstein; the Dutch American photography duo Inez van Lamsweerde and Vinoodh Matadin; and T’s creative director, Patrick Li — considered the merits of about 60 pictures they’d nominated, spanning roughly 100 years. (Ahead of time, each panelist was asked to submit 10 nominations, and they weren’t allowed to choose ones they’d worked on.)

As one might expect, there were many arguments. Evangelista petitioned not to include “Priscilla,” Szabo’s 1969 portrait of a girl smoking alone at New York’s Jones Beach, since the subject, she reasoned, looks too young to have consented. Matadin unwittingly eliminated a famous album cover with a casual observation — “It was clearly based on a work of ours” — and Li talked about how graphic design can make an image more indelible. Although diversity was a consideration, most of the photographs they included orbit global fashion capitals (New York, London, Milan, Paris and Tokyo), with an emphasis on New York, where several of the panelists live.

In the end, the following list, which isn’t ranked — it’s presented roughly in the order in which the entries were discussed — can be only so definitive. The selections are bound by the preferences and personal experiences of the judges, or even by their moods that day. But there’s one thing they could all agree on: Whether you think it’s art or frivolity, fashion photography is about so much more than clothes. — *Nick Haramis*

## 1\. Richard Avedon, Brooke Shields for Calvin Klein Jeans, 1980

![Brooke Shields, wearing a maroon shirt, blue jeans and black boots, stands in front of a gray background. She is leaning forward, with her right knee bent, and both of her hands are holding her shirt.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-02/08tmag-fashionimages_01-02-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

A photo from Richard Avedon’s 1980 Calvin Klein Jeans campaign featuring the actress Brooke Shields. Credit... Photograph by Richard Avedon. Copyright © The Richard Avedon Foundation

When the American actress Brooke Shields was 15, [her appearance](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/12/02/t-magazine/fashion/brooke-shields-calvin-klein-jeans-ad-eighties.html) in a 1980 Calvin Klein Jeans campaign brought the controversy already swirling around her — about the sexualization of minors in the media, largely on account of her starring role, at age 12, as a child coerced into prostitution in Louis Malle’s 1978 film, “Pretty Baby” — to a fever pitch. Taken by the American fashion photographer Richard Avedon, one of the series’s most enduring images features Shields, her hip cocked and her lined eyes trained on the camera, wearing tight denim and a silky maroon top that she might be buttoning or unbuttoning. Even the fact that the picture is in color feels like a concession to sensuality for Avedon (who died in 2004), as he typically worked in black and white. The idea for the ads, he told an interviewer years later, was to make them “completely quotable,” which led to a script by the copywriter Doon Arbus (daughter of Diane, also included on this list) that was filled with double entendres. The most provocative line in the TV commercials — “You want to know what comes between me and my Calvins? Nothing.” — implied that Shields wasn’t wearing underwear. (She’s since said she didn’t understand the innuendo.) The marketing’s reception underscored the public’s capacity for moral outrage while also proving that sex sells: Even as networks pulled the ads, demand for the jeans skyrocketed. — *Kate Guadagnino*

![Two billboards.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_02-03/08tmag-fashionimages_02-03-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Calvin Klein Jeans billboards in New York in the 1980s. Credit... Randy Mendelsohn

**Ian Bradley:** This was a milestone celebrity endorsement for the brand.

**Cate Holstein:** Calvin paved the way for so many other campaigns, and this was his first big one.

**Patrick Li:** As I was putting together my list, I kept thinking about how the best images either reflect a moment or foretell the future. For me, that’s most evident in Avedon’s [Andy Warhol mural](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/284262) \[a 1969 black-and-white triptych of the artist and his creative circle\], which celebrated the downtown New York art scene of that era and ushered in a new approach to group photography by collaging together several different shots.

**Linda Evangelista:** Prior to becoming a model, I worked at a 7-Eleven-type store in St. Catharines, Ontario. All I did was buy magazines and fantasize about getting out of my hometown to model. Avedon’s pictures made me *dream*. I could be wrong, but I believe he shot his fashion on a gray background and his nonfashion on a white background, so I don’t know if the Warhol mural is considered fashion. I know this because I did a \[spring 1994\] Versace campaign with him. I’d just had lung surgery and was drugged, but my agents convinced me to do it. I asked if he’d shoot it on a white background, and he was fed up with me, so he did, but he’d also promised to retouch my nipples and didn’t.

**Holstein:** When I think of Avedon, his [1957 portrait of Marilyn Monroe](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/128614) is the first that comes to mind. I don’t know that anybody’s ever seen a photo of someone looking so glamorous yet sad. And it would seem to inspire Steven Meisel’s Versace campaigns.

**Li:** That one also speaks to a moment before photo approvals, because Monroe’s PR person would never have approved it.

**Inez** **van Lamsweerde:** Vinoodh and I ended up choosing this Brooke Shields picture.

**Li:** Which, not for nothing, has a gray background.

## 2\. Guy Bourdin, “Woman With a Portrait of John Travolta,” 1979

![A model leans back over the arm of a sofa, wearing a shiny green dress and tights. A framed picture of John Travolta is set on a pink carpet by her feet.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-14/08tmag-fashionimages_01-14-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Guy Bourdin’s “Woman With a Portrait of John Travolta,” from the spring 1979 Charles Jourdan campaign. Credit... © The Guy Bourdin Estate, 2026. Courtesy of Louise Alexander Gallery

In “Saturday Night Fever” (1977), John Travolta’s Tony Manero is the king of his local disco in Bay Ridge, Brooklyn, and a typical pop culture-steeped teen, with posters of Bruce Lee, Al Pacino and Farrah Fawcett hanging in his bedroom. Two years after the movie’s release, the French photographer [Guy Bourdin](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/01/22/fashion/guy-bourdins-work-stands-the-test-of-time.html) came out with his own, more emphatic depiction of celebrity worship, casting Travolta, who was by then a global superstar, as the object of desire. In this image — an ad for the now-defunct French shoe label Charles Jourdan, with which the exacting Bourdin, who died in 1991, had a long relationship — a model does a backbend onto a sofa, with a framed photo of Travolta propped between her legs. She might be offering herself up to the actor, or perhaps we’re catching her midswoon. Bourdin’s much-imitated pictures were known for their voyeuristic and surreal qualities (he was a protégé of the American French Surrealist Man Ray, whom he met in the early 1950s), and for their attention to composition and color. Here, the saturated palette — the green and red of the woman’s satiny dress and sash combined with the hot pink and jet black of the carpet and couch — contributes to the sense that we’ve entered a bewitching other world. *— K.G.*

![A man and a woman, under the glare of bright lights, dance in a studio space.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_09-06/08tmag-fashionimages_09-04-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Bourdin, photographed with a model at his Paris studio circa 1975. Credit... David Hurn/Magnum Photos

**Van Lamsweerde:** We own this picture; it’s in our home. The way he incorporated a black-and-white photo into the color image is unbelievable. There’s something about the disturbance of the color saturation with that image of John, and the fact that he’s looking at the camera and she’s not.

**Vinoodh Matadin:** It was from a time when they had three months to take one series. Now we do 30 pictures in half an hour.

**Nick Haramis:** Ian and Patrick, you each nominated a different image from Bourdin’s collaboration with Charles Jourdan.

**Li:** I’m not sure a lot of these images could be made today.

**Bradley:** So much of it would be social media [outrage bait](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2021/photographs-2/charles-jourdan-autumn-1979).

**Li:** But that doesn’t diminish its beauty or influence.

## 3\. Hans Feurer, Susan Hess for Comme des Garçons, 1983

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-15/08tmag-fashionimages_01-15-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

A picture by Hans Feurer of the model Susan Hess for a 1983 Comme des Garçons catalog. Credit... Hans Feurer/Trunk Archive

Around the time that [Rei Kawakubo’s Comme des Garçons](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/09/03/t-magazine/rei-kawakubo-comme-des-garcons-menswear.html) was upending the notion of what fashion could be with its deconstructed, unfinished and asymmetrical clothes, this image challenged what a fashion photograph could look like. Taken in 1983 for the Japanese brand’s catalog, it encapsulates many of Kawakubo’s fundamental ideas: the silhouette of the black dress obscures, rather than accentuates, the subject’s body; the scarf is wrapped around her face. And just as Comme des Garçons went from being disregarded — critics had derided its 1981 Paris debut as “Hiroshima chic” — to a foundational influence for generations of designers, the kinetic abstraction of this photograph became a touchstone for editorials. (It’s hard to imagine an issue of American Vogue without a model leaping through the air.) The project was a departure for the Swiss photographer Hans Feurer, who died in 2024; his work often depicted athletic women in exuberant colors or settings. And though the American model Susan Hess worked with many of the period’s top designers, she’d eventually leave the industry to pursue an acting and filmmaking career. Nevertheless, their collaboration remains an indelible souvenir of a moment when fashion made room for designers who weren’t following trends but defying them outright. — *Eviana Hartman*

**Bradley:** It helped ground the avant-garde by making it joyful.

**Haramis:** It’s interesting that we read joy into this picture given that you can’t see the model’s face.

**Bradley:** It’s in the body.

**Matadin:** For me, this is about total freedom and pushing against the establishment. In that way, it’s a perfect image for the brand.

**Holstein:** It also marks the beginning of what we know as the 1980s — the wackiness, the happiness.

**Evangelista:** What I loved about Hans is that unlike so many photographers, he wasn’t afraid of fashion. But he used a very long lens to blow out the background, so he couldn’t direct. Like, you couldn’t hear him because you were so far away.

**Van Lamsweerde:** He was such a master at capturing a mistake — this is a lucky shot, but you must get the right lucky shot.

## 4\. Nick Knight, Devon Aoki for Visionaire, 1997

![Devon Aoki wears an embroidered peach dress with a raised tube-shaped collar. Her left eye is teal, and she has a flower pin and a safety pin attached to her forehead.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-10/08tmag-fashionimages_01-10-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

A portrait by Nick Knight of the model Devon Aoki for a 1997 edition of Visionaire magazine. Credit... Nick Knight

The British photographer Nick Knight, 67, known for his work with brands like Jil Sander and Yohji Yamamoto, was early to adopt digital manipulation, embracing programs like Quantel’s Paintbox years before the widespread reliance on Photoshop. One of his most celebrated images, made in 1997 for the 20th edition of Visionaire magazine, features the American model Devon Aoki in a cowl-neck, pink satin brocade dress from Alexander McQueen’s spring 1997 collection. Her left eye is a milky shade of blue (it’s a contact lens), and her forehead has been x-ed out with an oversize safety pin and what appears to be a deep gash full of pink flowers, details added in postproduction. “I like the idea of the fragility and strength mixed together,” [Knight said in 2020](https://www.showstudio.com/news/surrealism-q-nick-knight) about this picture, which not only presaged the rise of AI editing tools but also inspired the iconic cover for the Icelandic singer Björk’s 1997 album, “Homogenic,” shot by Knight and art-directed by McQueen. — *Jameson Montgomery*

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_02-14/08tmag-fashionimages_02-14-mobileMasterAt3x-v3.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Knight, photographed in London in 1997. Credit... Nick Knight

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_bjork/08tmag-fashionimages_bjork-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

The cover of Björk’s 1997 album, “Homogenic,” shot by Knight and art-directed by the fashion designer Alexander McQueen. Credit... OLI Records

**Bradley:** Nick Knight *is* technology. He predicted the future of Photoshop and altering images, and he’s had such memorable collaborations with designers.

**Holstein:** To me, fashion is more of a concept than anything. It’s about pushing things forward and taking us into a new era. This one brings us into the 2000s. And to be shooting with Devon at the time …

**Bradley:** She was *the* face.

**Holstein:** And she reflected a new way of looking at beauty.

## 5\. Robert Mapplethorpe, “Patti Smith,” 1975

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_top25-02/08tmag-fashionimages_top25-02-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

A 1975 portrait by Robert Mapplethorpe of the musician and writer Patti Smith, which became the cover for her debut album, “Horses.” Credit... “Patti Smith,” 1975 © Robert Mapplethorpe Foundation. Used by permission

The American photographer [Robert Mapplethorpe](https://www.nytimes.com/topic/person/robert-mapplethorpe) sometimes used the curator Sam Wagstaff’s white-walled Greenwich Village penthouse as a photo studio, and it was there, in 1975, that he shot [Patti Smith](https://archive.nytimes.com/tmagazine.blogs.nytimes.com/2011/10/14/requiem-lass/) for the cover of her debut album, “Horses.” Lovers turned friends, Mapplethorpe and Smith were each other’s muses, and though they might have harbored different ideas about glamour ­— later in his career, Mapplethorpe, who died in 1989 at age 42, started taking pictures of celebrities and society types — it was a raw, insouciant vision that won out that day. One side of Smith’s face is bathed in natural light as she stares at the lens; the cuffs of her white shirt have been cut off, a ribbon hangs from her neck like an unknotted tie and she has a men’s suit jacket slung over her shoulder. [As Smith said](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/130889380) in a 2010 interview, “It has a little bit of Baudelaire, a little bit of Catholic boy, a little bit of Frank Sinatra and a lot of Robert.” Arista Records executives pleaded with Smith to allow an editor to airbrush the image, but she refused. And just as the poetic punk of “Horses” was a revelation, so was its cover, which introduced those beyond Lower Manhattan to a new photographic talent and to one of the greatest androgynous dressers: this self-possessed woman who redefined rocker chic. — *K.G.*

<video src="https://vp.nyt.com/video/2026/09/03/177850_1_08tmag-fashionimages_video_Mapplethorpe_wg_1080p.mp4" controls=""></video>

Mapplethorpe in his 1978 short film about Smith, “Still Moving.” Credit Credit... “Still Moving/Patti Smith,” 1978 © Robert Mapplethorpe Foundation. Used by permission

**Holstein:** Talk about changing the idea of beauty.

**Li:** This was taken at such an important moment in the women’s movement. It’s interesting to compare the kind of undone androgyny of this photo with Helmut Newton’s work at the time: two sides of that same idea. To me, this is a forever image that’s influenced not just fashion but the culture.

**Haramis:** We can’t discuss influence without acknowledging the relationship between fashion, photography and music. Many of the group’s nominations — Joseph Cultice’s work with Marilyn Manson, Jean-Paul Goude’s portraits of Grace Jones — were album covers.

**Bradley:** Maybe that’s why this picture feels so liberating — because of the music we associate with it.

**Holstein:** A lot of Mapplethorpe’s work felt that way. And you can see the influence he’d have on \[the French fashion designer and photographer\] Hedi Slimane.

## 6\. Helmut Newton, “Yves Saint Laurent, Rue Aubriot, French Vogue, Paris 1975”

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-11/08tmag-fashionimages_01-11-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Helmut Newton’s “Yves Saint Laurent, Rue Aubriot, French Vogue, Paris 1975.” Credit... Helmut Newton Foundation/Trunk Archive

Dubbed the “King of Kink” by the press, the German photographer [Helmut Newton](https://www.nytimes.com/2004/01/24/nyregion/helmut-newton-fashion-photographer-83.html) may be best known for his provocative images of women in various states of undress. But in one of his most emblematic shots, the subject is fully clothed. For French Vogue in 1975, Newton photographed the Danish model Vibeke Knudsen standing under a streetlight near his apartment in Paris’s Fourth Arrondissement. Capturing Knudsen in black and white, smoking a cigarette in a pinstriped Yves Saint Laurent tuxedo with slicked-back hair, Newton presented a deeply sexy version of androgyny. “I’m fascinated by ambiguity in a woman,” he told [The Times in 1998](https://www.nytimes.com/1998/02/22/magazine/flashback.html). “There’s something of a boy in a certain kind of woman.” While Saint Laurent’s designs inspired photographers from Avedon to Irving Penn, his relationship with Newton — with whom he collaborated on many campaigns and look books — was particularly fruitful, born out of mutual admiration and a shared aesthetic. “Saint Laurent inspired me to take some of my best fashion photos,” said Newton, who died in a car accident in 2004. “There are so few people with whom I completely agree.” — *Kin Woo*

**Li:** This is undisputed. We don’t need to waste time talking about it. The fact that this was taken around the same time as Smith’s portrait is meaningful.

**Matadin:** It reminds you that history has different narratives.

**Li:** And it’s a bit ambiguous. You don’t know exactly what’s happening.

**Bradley:** It’s chic and queer.

## 7\. Steven Meisel, “Makeover Madness” for Italian Vogue, 2005

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-05/08tmag-fashionimages_01-05-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

An image from Steven Meisel’s “Makeover Madness” editorial for Italian Vogue’s July 2005 issue. Credit... Steven Meisel

A bruised, bedridden woman writhes in agony (or ecstasy?) as a nurse unpeels the bandage from her face. Another woman, her oversize sunglasses held in place by her cranial cocoon, seems oblivious to the ordeal behind her, fixated on either her reflection or her phone. A third patient, in a fluffy shrug, assesses her pharmaceuticals, served like afternoon tea on a silver platter. The American photographer Steven Meisel, now 72, seems to be saying that beauty is a battlefield. What was provocative in 2005, when “Makeover Madness” was published on the cover of Italian Vogue — and across 80 pages inside the magazine — is almost banal to our eyes today, making it one of the most prescient images from the aughts. (It’s hard to imagine [Balenciaga’s spring 2020](https://wwd.com/fashion-news/shows-reviews/gallery/balenciaga-rtw-spring-1203324873/balenciaga-rtw-spring-2020/) facial prosthetics, for example, existing without these pictures.) This series, which features Evangelista plus a handful of other models and includes depictions of liposuction, breast implant surgery and facial injections, eerily anticipated our current era, in which influencers document their surgical journeys to Seoul and getting a face-lift is a status symbol rather than a secret. — *E.H.*

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_02-17/08tmag-fashionimages_02-17-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

The issue’s foldout cover. Credit... © Steven Meisel, Vogue Italia

**Li:** I know we weren’t supposed to nominate the work of anyone here, but it’s too hard not to. I love this series so much. Linda, I’m curious about your experience.

**Evangelista:** Steven sometimes picks up on current events. There was so much buzz at the time about plastic surgery. It was the beginning of it becoming less hidden. And it was glamorous because you’d go to a hotel to heal. During those Franca days \[Franca Sozzani was the magazine’s editor in chief from 1988 until her death in 2016\], there weren’t a lot of preproduction meetings. We just guessed where to draw the surgical marks in Sharpie. It was a total spoof on plastic surgery, but he made it beautiful and glamorous. For me, that’s always been so important when shooting fashion: If you’re making a face, if you’re crying, if you’re screaming, if you’re in ugly clothes, if you’re being tortured — no matter what the situation — the subject still must be beautiful. And I think he managed that.

**Li:** I would argue, too, that this work is about bringing taboos to light. That’s how the culture moves forward.

**Evangelista:** This had foreshadowing in it. I remember people said, “Why would you do it?” And I was like, “Why wouldn’t I?”

**Van Lamsweerde:** What I find astounding about this period of Meisel’s work is that it’s all so perfect, even the tragedy.

## 8\. Steven Meisel, “Anglo-Saxon Attitude” for British Vogue, 1993

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-06/08tmag-fashionimages_01-06-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

A portrait of the model Stella Tennant from Meisel’s “Anglo-Saxon Attitude” editorial for British Vogue’s December 1993 issue. Credit... Steven Meisel

In the summer of 1993, Stella Tennant, a 23-year-old student at England’s Winchester School of Art, was scouted by the fashion editor Isabella Blow to star amid a new crop of models in a portfolio photographed by Meisel for the December issue of British Vogue. It was Tennant’s first modeling job and, despite making a lackluster entrance — “Stella came off the train from Scotland smelling of goats,” [Blow later told the writer Hamish Bowles](https://www.vogue.com/article/a-tribute-to-stella-tennant) — she enchanted Meisel, who’d go on to cast her in a Versace campaign the following week. Meisel’s portrait of Tennant, then a boyish young woman with short, shaggy hair, black-rimmed eyes and a pierced septum, helped kick-start a decades-long career that lasted until her death in 2020, at age 50. Though she’d never become a household name, Tennant’s patrician elegance and chameleonic quality made her a favorite of designers like Phoebe Philo and Helmut Lang, as well as influential photographers including Arthur Elgort, Paolo Roversi and Tim Walker. “I fell in love with Stella,” Meisel recalled of that initial encounter. “She gave everything and I adored her.” — *K.W.*

**Evangelista:** For a couple of the photos I chose, it was like the photographer was presenting someone to you. It’s the same with \[the American photographer\] Peter Beard and \[the Somali American model\] Iman. This was Steven saying, “I’m giving you a gift, and she’s going to rock your world.” I worked with Stella on her second shoot, for Versace, and I felt so uncool around her. She was shy and timid but so damn cool.

**Holstein:** Once again, this changed beauty standards. It defined an entire generation.

**Bradley:** It was a new proposal for what the modern It girl could be.

**Matadin:** A big part of Steven’s legacy is finding the girl and making her.

## 9\. Irving Penn, “Large Sleeve (Sunny Harnett), New York,” 1951

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-16/08tmag-fashionimages_01-16-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Irving Penn’s “Large Sleeve (Sunny Harnett), New York” (1951). Credit... © The Irving Penn Foundation

The American photographer Irving Penn never stopped working. He spent more than six decades taking pictures, beginning in the 1940s, soon after he was hired at Vogue (initially as a graphic designer), and continuing right up until his death, at age 92, in 2009. His work, which includes 165 Vogue covers (more, by far, than any other photographer), is notable for its visual clarity and artful composition, with many of his images cropped in unexpected ways — a shift from the traditionally centered photos that’d long dominated fashion magazines. In one, from 1951, the American model Sunny Harnett rests an arm atop a table, the billowing sleeve of her blouse taking up most of the frame. Though Harnett’s face is visible, Penn opted to cut the image at her eye, shifting focus to the pin tucking and lace detailing of her top. A gelatin silver print of the photo is now in the permanent collection at New York’s Museum of Modern Art, which described it in a 2019 catalog as “so exquisitely balanced that we accept as inevitable the photographer’s daring cropping of the model’s head.” — *J.M.*

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_02/08tmag-fashionimages_02-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Penn, photographed at his New York studio circa 1954. Credit... ©The Irving Penn Foundation

**Li:** I’ve never seen an image that celebrates a sleeve as much as this does. If we’re talking about fashion, it’s hard not to include something like this. The crop of the photo, just at the eye — there’s something so weird and transcendent about it.

**Van Lamsweerde:** Yeah. It’s incredible.

**Evangelista:** I nominated a Penn [photograph of his wife](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/144834/rochas-mermaid-dress-lisa-fonssagrives-penn-paris) \[the Swedish model Lisa Fonssagrives\]. I love a wife as a muse. They did so much work together, and he was so crazy in love with her.

**Li:** Penn’s work with Lisa is indelible. But as a single photo, this —

**Van Lamsweerde:** It sums Penn up.

**Evangelista:** But every Penn photo sums Penn up. A cigarette butt does. Flowers do.

## 10\. David LaChapelle, “Tupac Shakur: Solitary II,” 1996

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/t-magazine/08tmag-fashionimages_01-13/08tmag-fashionimages_01-13-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

A 1996 portrait by David LaChapelle of the rapper Tupac Shakur for Details magazine. Credit... © David LaChapelle. Courtesy of Staley-Wise Gallery, New York

The American photographer [David LaChapelle](https://www.nytimes.com/2011/05/29/fashion/david-lachapelle-from-photographer-to-artist.html), 63, redefined the celebrity portrait, shooting entertainers and world leaders ranging from Lil’ Kim to Hillary Clinton on surreal, cinematic sets that often blend religious iconography with Baroque or Renaissance motifs. In 1996, New York magazine called him “the Fellini of Photography.” LaChapelle came of age in 1980s Manhattan, starting out as a photographer for Interview magazine before shooting for Vanity Fair and Rolling Stone. One of his most unforgettable images is from a 1996 commission for Details magazine featuring the American rapper Tupac Shakur, then 24. “Solitary II,” as the image is titled, features the hip-hop star — who’d recently been released after serving a prison sentence for sexual abuse — adorned with heavy gold jewelry, a “Thug Life” tattoo across his abdomen, reclining in a bathtub. The image could be read as suggesting that the arts-school-educated Shakur (he majored in theater) was washing away his violent past in preparation for a creative rebirth. Instead, five months later, [he was killed](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/31/arts/music/tupac-shakur-murder.html) in a drive-by shooting in Las Vegas. — *K.W.*

**Bradley:** This Tupac series represents hip-hop moving into the world of luxury fashion. He walked in a Romeo Gigli men’s wear show shortly after this. It was right before his death; his security guards walked the runway too.

**Haramis:** At this point, mainstream rap was still largely homophobic. But with this image, LaChapelle almost seems to be encouraging Tupac to embrace a certain kind of queerness, or softness at least.

**Bradley:** Yeah, he’s playing that up. Tupac got a lot of flak for this.

**Van Lamsweerde:** It’s fabulous.

## 11\. Deana Lawson, “Binky & Tony Forever,” 2009

![](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/08/slideshows/08tmag-fashionimages_slid-slide-H93U/08tmag-fashionimages_slid-slide-H93U-mobileMasterAt3x.jpg?quality=75&auto=avif&disable=upscale&width=1800)

Deana Lawson’s “Binky & Tony Forever” (2009). Credit... © Deana Lawson. Courtesy of the artist and Gagosian

Tony sits on the edge of a bed decorated with a gold satin bedspread. Binky stands between his legs, her arms coiled around his. An oval mirror reflects a sliver of the scene from another angle, suggesting a world beyond the frame. The moment appears spontaneous, but like all of [Deana Lawson’s](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/05/05/magazine/deana-lawson.html) work, it was carefully composed and meticulously directed. Lawson, 47, who was born and raised in Rochester, New York, made “Binky & Tony Forever” in 2009, when she was in her 20s and living in Bedford-Stuyvesant, Brooklyn. She’d met Binky, a makeup artist, on a shoot and invited her, along with her friend Anthony, to pose in the photographer’s own bedroom; she wanted the image, which was later used as the album cover for Blood Orange’s “Freetown Sound” (2016), to capture young Black love. The photograph is part of her broader project to build an expansive portrait of the Black diaspora, uncovering moments of beauty and grace within the everyday spaces of working-class life. As the novelist [Zadie Smith wrote](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2018/05/07/deana-lawsons-kingdom-of-restored-glory) in The New Yorker in 2018, “Outside a Lawson portrait you might be working three jobs, just keeping your head above water, struggling. But inside her frame you are beautiful, imperious, unbroken, unfallen.” — *Elly Fishman*

**Van Lamsweerde:** Oh, this picture’s so good.

**Bradley:** Deana’s a fine artist, but I think fashion photography in the last five years has become a lot like this.

**Li:** It’s on every mood board.

**Bradley:** And these homey interiors — I love this set.

**Li:** I don’t think these *are* sets.
