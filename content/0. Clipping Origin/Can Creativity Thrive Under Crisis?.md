**written by** DANYA ISSAWI  
  
Decade after decade, Lebanon and its people have found themselves on the receiving end of Israeli attacks, first framed as targeting the PLO, and later, Hezbollah. In 1978, 1982, 1993, 1996, 2006, 2023, 2024, 2025 and, now, 2026 (with intermittent incursions in between), this tiny country has been under bombardment from below its southern border, holding on as ancient heritage sites are reduced to rubble, entire villages are razed, and loved ones are forcibly displaced and disappeared.  
  
Since March, over 4,200 people have been killed and over 12,000 wounded by Israeli attacks, according to Lebanon’s Ministry of Public Health. Despite it all, the Lebanese live on. The Red Cross and Red Crescent pull children from the rubble as the bakers cook manoushe over a saj, as the hairstylists paint blonde highlights into someone’s locks, as the taxi drivers dangle cigarettes out their windows, as the priests begin a sermon, and as the artists, the designers, the photographers, the painters, the writers make art. Creative expression might seem trivial as bombs rain down on one’s hometown, on one’s family, on the people and places they love. But for many, it’s all they can do to keep moving forward. For these individuals, art is just as much a mode of self-expression as it is an act of cultural preservation in the face of erasure and occupation.  
  
Four Lebanese creatives sat down with Danya Issawi to explore the artist’s place in times of strife. Below, fashion designers Cynthia Merhej and Ahmed Amer, photographer and art director Michèle Aoun, and visual artist and photographer Yasmina Hilal discuss how living under siege has impacted their work, how they support one another and their communities, and what role art has in a nation under bombardment.

**DANYA ISSAWI: During more “typical” times, what is your creative circle like here in Lebanon?**  
  
**CYNTHIA MERJEH (Fashion Designer):** It’s an intergenerational circle. And in Beirut, it’s not only the people who are practising something creative. You have the periphery—the people who are just generally interested in that world, that are hanging out with artists or being patrons. It’s all quite interconnected in a way. There isn’t a very competitive vibe.  
  
**DI: What has it been like for you all to continue your creative practices during this most recent Israeli aggression?**  
  
**MICHÈLE AOUN (Art Director):** It’s been years of this. Honestly, we just survive. We go on. We laugh about it, even though deep inside we’re obviously traumatised. Like, we’re *really* traumatised, and it’s not normal to go on living this way, but we still do it—not because we’re resilient… We don’t have any other choice and we love being here.  
  
**YASMINA HILAL (Photographer):** It’s always a big shock, but then you kind of get accustomed to it, unfortunately. I try to create as much as I can. It’s a form of resisting, or being able to say, “I’m going to continue, I’m going to create, I’m going to keep going.” What’s interesting is how adaptable we are.  
  
**AHMED AMER (Fashion Designer):** The situation here is bad for creativity, but at the same time, I feel like being in Beirut, being around all this hardship. It pushes me. You will only be happy or satisfied if you create—you can’t live without creating. You have to be addicted to creating to exist in such a situation. I was born during a war. \[Creating\] is a way to just let go.  
  
**YH:** It’s a way to escape, where I can detach, where I can talk to myself and reflect. We live in survival mode, but we create to survive.  
  
**MA:** In Beirut, you can feel the community everywhere, because Lebanon is like a village. Sometimes I’m tired of saying hi to each person on the street, but at the same time, this is what keeps us going. We underestimate the power of community, of having such a tiny country.  
  
**CM:** There’s a lot of amazing stuff happening in Beirut that probably no one else in the world knows about. It’s so exciting for me to just go to the Beirut Art Center, see this Lebanese curator or this furniture designer who’s done this amazing pop-up—or to go see what the fuck Ahmed is doing at his pop-up, or visit Yasmina’s studio. The fact that we all know each other makes it even more exciting. \[It’s like\] Paris in the ’20s and the ’30s, when Picasso and Modigliani and all these artists would just be hanging out; they all go to the bar and they bump into each other. That’s kind of why you get sucked into the vortex when you’re in Beirut. You’re like, “I don’t really need to go anywhere else. Everything is here.”  
  
That said, sometimes I think, “Okay, well, what if we didn’t have this as a constant?” Every year, something horrible happens that we just have to brush past. Where would our careers be relative to our Western peers? When I think of people whose parents moved during one of the wars to America… Like, that could have been my parents. Where would I be now? And would I ever wish for that?  
  
**YH:** There’s a certain gap in Lebanon when it comes to the creative fields and funding and exposure. The Gulf region gets a lot of that, but we share strong work that doesn’t really get to be seen as much.  
  
**AA:** It should be a main pillar in the government. There’s a lot of initiative, but there’s not a lot of official support. You’re always asking for sponsors and the funding has turned toward the main needs, like food. These creative institutions turn into emergency organisations.  
  
**CM:** Yeah. A lot of art-based institutions, like the Beirut Art Center, have become like NGOs. You go and drop clothes off and make donations.  
  
**MA:** As a creative community, we have a responsibility to turn the art spaces into shelters. It’s beautiful for us to do something for others. It’s true we don’t have funding from the government, but we cannot deny that people are making great efforts that we need to be proud of.  
  
**AA:** The art scene and the queer community are always offering change and working toward something better.  
  
**YH:** The core of it all is the belief in being there. The love we have for this place. It’s the idea that we continue and we invest, even though people will tell us, “Don’t invest in Lebanon. Leave the country.” This is our happiness. This is where we feel the most inspired. This is where our friends, families, our culture, our ancestors are. It’s hard to think of letting go of a place, of the fact that someone is going to try to occupy it. It makes it even more intentional for us to stay.

**DI: You’ve all talked about how chaos inspires you. I’m curious how the chaos of this recent season of bombardment and occupation shows up in your work.**  
  
**YH:** Identity politics does play a role. Recently, my work is a lot about the past and how we can keep it. A lot of artisanal work has become so sacred, especially in the South. Preservation is super important. There is a lot of erasure happening in our country.  
  
**CM:** When I look, for example, at Michèle’s work—every day, she’s taking these amazing photos of things she sees around the city. For me, that’s incredibly important work, because as Yasmina is saying, preservation seems to be a constant theme. Now it feels like it’s even bigger, because we’ve seen what they’ve done in Gaza. It’s literally at our doorstep now. They’re just erasing whole villages. When I see Michèle’s photos, there’s something about it that is very soothing in a weird way—because you’re like, “Wow, this place still exists. It’s still beautiful in the way I remember it, and it’s so soft in the ways that I remember it.”  
  
**MA:** I’m from the South, and I spent so much time in the South as a young girl. I remember the Israeli occupation very well. I was telling my boyfriend two days ago that it’s crazy how our country is changing. I feel that I’m attached to these things—the little shops and random stuff. I’m constantly melancholic about a better past.  
  
It’s also about people who care a lot about capitalism—ugly buildings in the city and stuff like that. Unfortunately, we find ourselves not just facing the Israeli occupation, but also Lebanese people who don’t care about preserving the gems that we have in our little cocoon. I think I have a bigger rage towards these people. They’re supposed to be one of us.

**DI: Some people looking externally might wonder: “Why would art be important at a time like this?” What would you say to that?**  
  
**AA:** For me, it’s the only way I can express myself. Art is an abstract language, and it’s a language everyone can relate to. They can relate emotionally.  
  
**CM:** It’s a way to feel alive and stay alive. It’s a way to process your own feelings. At the end of the day, it’s about human contact and connection that is really vital and valuable. This aliveness comes not just from a selfish place, like, “I need to create things because I want to make something pretty.” No—I want to create something because I want someone to relate to me, whether they’re in Lebanon and going through the same shit, or in America \[and might gain\] some understanding of my situation here, and vote differently or do something that can change things. It’s all about this hope that you can incite change solely through that. I truly believe artists are always the drivers of change in every great movement or revolution.

**DI: What’s your hope for your generation of Lebanese and Arab creatives? And what’s your hope for generations after that?**  
  
**MA:** Support for each other is something I really want to grow. We need each other, and not just in Lebanon. There’s something stronger happening in the Arab world: People finally understand what it’s like to be under occupation. Five years ago, nobody really knew what they know today about what happened to the people of Gaza. Nobody really knew what we’ve been going through. It’s important to feel seen by the West, but it’s not a big deal for me. I don’t really care to be understood by the West. My people are the Arab world. That means a lot to me.  
  
**CM:** Everything that’s happened since 2023 with Gaza… It was totally a watershed moment. People do finally care. But I don’t care about that validation from the West anymore. I think that’s a huge deal. This has been kind of drilled into our heads, not just as Lebanese, but regionally—like, “We need to have the approval of these people that colonised us for us to be considered civilised or worthy or whatever.” There’s just so much interesting shit happening in Beirut or Cairo or Casablanca or Jeddah that you’re like, “Why do I care, really, what’s happening abroad?”  
  
We don’t have these links between us yet, because there’s constant war and displacement. My dream is more freedom of travel between our nations, and more meetings of minds. More and more people will realise we have something great going on here. At the same time, at what cost is stability going to come to us? The political reality is terrifying. We cannot forget what’s actually happening. Is stability going to come at the cost of normalisation, occupation, more killing, more wiping out the South? Who knows?  
  
**YH:** It’s a bit of a paradox. It’s a weird feeling, because my three family members in the South lost their homes. My great-grandmother’s house, 100 years old, was destroyed, demolished. That was the hardest week, and then I travelled because I’m showing my work in Europe, and I had to get accustomed to how life goes on. All I’m thinking about is how much my family has lost.  
  
A lot of us, from the beginning of the war, are always on our phones. Cynthia knows how much of a breakdown I had. It’s information overload. There’s several nights I didn’t sleep because I was just looking at warfare material. I just feel really scared. How many false ceasefires have happened so far? There’s no belief in any of this stuff anymore.  
  
**CM:** I stopped watching the news to preserve my peace of mind, but also because, sadly, I literally can’t do anything. If something horrible is gonna happen, I’m gonna hear about it from my friends or my family. That’s the only news source I need. You have to find some kind of balance, otherwise you can’t function.  
  
**YH:** I’ve asked a lot of my friends, “How do you cope? How do you cry?” For me, it wasn’t until the third week that I burst. It’s just crazy to think there is no apparatus for measuring what type of pain we’re going through, and how we’re coping with such grief and death.  
  
**AA:** It’s an in-between. I can’t express like I used to express emotionally. Even while I’m on this call, my family in the South is chatting in the group, saying their town is under threat. I haven’t been back to the South since last year because I don’t want to relive this image. The funny thing is, my family cares about me being happy. They’re in a worse situation than me, and they’re checking in if I’m living and creating.  
  
**CM:** Art used to be a good way to detach, but now I don’t know if it’s enough. I have to detach to detach to detach so that I can detach into my work. We’re on a master level of detachment.  
  
**AA:** My emotions feel deformed.  
  
**CM:** When you don’t recognise your own feelings, how are you supposed to put them into your creative work? There’s no feeling that can describe seeing a whole village being razed, especially when you visit those places, you know those places. I don’t know how humans are supposed to process that. At the same time, there’s this feeling of gratitude constantly. I’m alive. I’m here. Our parents are telling us to make the most of it. I really am trying.  
  
**YH:** As a people, we always try to find pockets of happiness within. It’s not all misery—our coast, our food, our people. I think that’s what we try to show, all of us, in our work. In the end, it’s all about love.
