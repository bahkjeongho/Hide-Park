> “I am wary of exhibitions that are too closely tied to brands. I prefer shows that focus on genuine artistic movements.”

A journalist for Quotidien and Le Monde, Marc Beaugé is the founder of l’étiquette, which he leads alongside Society and So Foot. Despite his busy schedule, he made time for Say Who to talk about his journey.

**Tell us about your upbringing.**

**Marc Beaugé:**

My childhood was fairly typical, with a decent, although not brilliant, schooling in the Parisian suburb of Rungis. Very early on, however, I developed a keen interest in the fields that would eventually become my areas of professional expertise: soccer, music, and fashion. This interest became more refined, especially during my teenage years, when I was around fifteen, as I became fascinated by English subcultures (lads, punks, mods, casuals), soccer, music, and the fashion styles associated with them. That is where my initial cultural foundation stems from, further nourished by influential works such as John King’s writings on soccer and its environment, as well as authors connected to English culture like McL. Wilson. Discovering Michel Houellebecq and his book _Whatever_ when I was around 17 was also a major literary revelation.

**And then, you started your career as a journalist.**

**Marc Beaugé:**

After studying history and journalism, I quickly co-founded So Foot with Franck Annese at the age of 21. Alongside soccer, I nurtured my interest in music and fashion, writing for Technikart about these topics, but also about a personal concept: the “softness” of suburban areas, inspired by my own experiences in peri-urban zones, where the lack of cultural outlets meant that you have to create your own. This concept described a socio-geographic reality rarely depicted in France, unlike in England with artists such as Mike Skinner of The Streets. I broke free from that environment, mainly by preparing for journalism school entrance exams. That’s when I discovered Paris’ cultural diversity: concerts, shops like Colette—my first visit at age 18 was an event that required careful outfit planning—and the art world, which I explored through reading the culture section of Les Inrockuptibles and meeting Franck. These experiences, such as collaborating with Les Inrockuptibles on a special issue of So Foot in 2004, were my gateway to the cultural scene (museums, parties, and encounters like the one with Mike Skinner).

I’ve always been very enthusiastic and driven, preferring to juggle multiple projects at once. After So Foot, I went on to work with Technikart, Les Inrockuptibles, GQ, L’Équipe, and now Le Monde and L’Équipe. Keeping these projects running in parallel made me feel like I was living several lives. I’ve even written articles for GQ about suits from the Vélodrome stadium during Marseille’s games. My approach has always been to connect different fields: music and fashion, for example, are deeply intertwined and are part of our culture.

**What does clothing mean to you?**

**Marc Beaugé:**

For me, clothing is a cultural item just like literature or cinema. Once stripped of marketing and pure consumerism, it tells the story of who we are. This conviction was the driving force behind my work for _GQ_ and _L’Équipe_, which aimed to promote the idea that clothing deserves to be studied and that stories can be told about it. My expertise has developed in a very empirical way, through experience, reading (Robert Elms, Alan Flusser, Tatiana de Tolstoy), and, above all, through conversations with industry professionals (tailors, vintage experts, designers). This obsession with clothing has become a profession, especially by applying journalistic rigor to it: prioritizing in-depth articles, conducting investigations, and going out into the field. It is this approach that makes _L’Équipe_ unique.

I even turned that passion into a reality by launching a hat brand, Larose Paris, about ten years ago, with Isaac Larose. That experience, —though it only lasted five years and ultimately ended because it became too time-consuming to manage alongside my other work—was an incredibly formative time. We worked closely with prestigious brands (Ami, Our Legacy, White Mountaineering), and I learned a great deal about materials, production, and industry processes, which has proved invaluable to my writing. I have also participated in numerous collaborations with L’Équipe (Weston, Horace). Today, leading the creative direction of a brand—combining visual creation, storytelling, and product development—is a goal that thrills me.

**What sort of art speaks to you the most?**

**Marc Beaugé:**

When it comes to my current cultural life, I’m frustrated that I don’t spend enough time on it as a regular spectator. Still, I try to set aside time for culture, especially by visiting exhibitions with my family (my two daughters, ages 10 and 15). We go to places such as the Quai Branly or the Musée d’Orsay, often for educational purposes. I also keep track of the upcoming exhibitions; I was recently struck by the Brancusi show at the Centre Pompidou (the face of his sculpture “La Muse endormie” really resonated with me) and by the work of photographer Marie-Hélène Desquer. I am wary, however, of exhibitions that are too closely tied to brands, as I feel that fashion tends to encroach too much on the cultural sphere. I prefer exhibitions focused on genuine artistic movements, such as the Memphis Group or the Impressionists.

I don’t visit galleries as much, even though my passion for contemporary art was sparked some twenty years ago by Matthew Barney’s “Cremaster” cycle at the Palais de Tokyo. Today, my choices are often guided by a desire to give my children a cultural foundation that values technique and craftsmanship over radical concepts. I also take them to less conventional places, like the Charvet shirt shop, for cultural experiences centered on clothing and fabrics.

Finally, I often find myself drawn back to Manchester culture. I lived there for a year when I was eight. This experience had a lasting cultural impact on me, even though I didn’t fully understand my passion for music (Oasis, The Smiths, Stone Roses) until much later. This is where I got my unshakeable cultural foundation. If I had to name just one album, it would be Crève-Cœur by Daniel Darc,undoubtedly my favorite.
