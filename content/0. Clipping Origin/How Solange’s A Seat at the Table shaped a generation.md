Solange: spring/summer 2018

[Solange](https://www.dazeddigital.com/solange) is among the rare artists whose public presence has evolved through so many distinct phases that each can feel like an entirely different persona. Though her famous last name thrust her into the spotlight at an early age, she has continually drawn people into her world through an ever-expanding range of creative pursuits. For some, their first encounter with her was on-screen, whether through the theme song for Disney Channel’s *The Proud Family* or her lead role in the Y2K classic *Bring It On: All or Nothing*. Others were introduced to her artistry through her acclaimed 2012 single “Losing You,” co-written and produced with Dev Hynes. Yet, while these earlier endeavours hinted at the breadth of her creative world, it was *ASATT* that brought that vision into full focus.

Serving as her third full-length album – following her teenage debut *Solo Star* and sophomore release *Sol-Angel and the Hadley St. Dreams* – the record marked a distinct sonic, visual and lyrical evolution. Executive produced by Raphael Saadiq, the project was written over an eight-year period and recorded across three years, primarily in New Iberia, Louisiana. It also brought together a wide-ranging cast of collaborators, including [Kelela](https://www.dazeddigital.com/kelela), [Lil Wayne](https://www.dazeddigital.com/lil-wayne), [Sampha](https://www.dazeddigital.com/sampha), Q-Tip, BJ the Chicago Kid and Kelly Rowland. Upon release, it debuted at number one on the Billboard 200 and earned Solange her first Grammy Award for Best R&B Performance for “Cranes in the Sky”, helping establish the album as a defining work in her career.

When she first announced the album, Solange described the project as one inspired by “identity, empowerment, independence, grief and healing”. Looking back, *A Seat at the Table* arrived amid a period of political instability and racial injustice in the US, as the [Black Lives Matter](https://www.dazeddigital.com/black-lives-matter) movement was gaining national momentum. The movement itself began in 2013, following the acquittal of George Zimmerman in the killing of Trayvon Martin, and expanded nationally in the years that followed. The year before the album’s release, after the Charleston church shooting in which a white supremacist gunman murdered nine Black worshippers, Solange tweeted: “Was already weary. Was already heavy hearted. Was already tired. Where can we be safe? Where can we be free? Where can we be black?” It was a question echoing far beyond her own experience, and one she would go on to explore throughout *ASATT*.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YTtrnDbOQAU)

The album’s opening track, “Rise”, inspired by the Ferguson and Baltimore protests, is followed by 20 other tracks that trace the messiness, beauty and complexities of Black womanhood. On “Weary”, she looks for embodiment (*“I’m going to look for my body, yeah I’ll be back real soon”*), and by the time the album reaches “Don’t Wish Me Well”, she is grounded enough to leave behind the places and parts of herself that no longer fit. Woven across the album are nine interludes. Far from mere transitions, these spoken-word excerpts – featuring reflections from her parents alongside hip-hop legend Master P – provide a grounding framework for the songs around them.

An exploration of Solange’s artistry is only complete when accounting for the full breadth of her creative expressions. Spanning performance art shows at world-renowned institutions like the Sydney Opera House and the Guggenheim Museum, alongside her initiatives through Saint Heron, her career is defined by a commitment to defying conventional expectations and a constant devotion to design and art that is present in all her work. Viewed as a kind of non-physical architecture, *ASATT* draws on many of those same ideas: a carefully constructed refuge from the chaos of the outside world.

Despite the mainstream success that brought her work to a much wider audience, Solange’s ability to articulate the intimate realities of Black womanhood has made *ASATT* an album in which Black women across different backgrounds and experiences have been able to recognise parts of themselves they may have been conditioned to hide. Below, to celebrate ten years since the album’s release, ten young Black women share what the album meant to them and why it still resonates a decade later.

## NIA SMITH, 22

*“To me, the album represents strength in knowing yourself and being completely self-assured. She’s so unapologetically herself, and there’s something really powerful about embracing your identity without feeling the need to make yourself smaller for anyone else. A lot of Black women see ourselves in her, and there’s something so affirming about seeing a Black woman embrace her identity so unapologetically, and the album’s celebration of Black womanhood and individuality is why it still resonates ten years on.”*

## HALIMA, 26

*“ASATT came out when I was 16. I had just started my A-Levels and was feeling super enraged by the murders of Black people at the hands of police in the UK, but particularly in the US. It felt scary to witness so much death in my teenage years and come to this horrifying realisation that my skin colour makes me a target for mistreatment and brutality. It made me depressed, but then I heard ASATT, an album made for Black people by a Black person. An album that is so unapologetically Black – that, amidst all the violence, made me feel proud and lucky to be Black.*

*“I love “Mad (feat. Lil Wayne)” – for obvious reasons. I’m mad as fuck most of the time, and racism sometimes means I need to quell those feelings to ensure nobody is misunderstanding or weaponising them against me. Anger is seen as such a negative emotion for Black people, but the song is a great reminder that I have the right to be mad as fuck at the state of the world and its treatment of Black people.*

*“Not only is it sonically a masterpiece, but it’s an album that doesn’t minimise Black women’s feelings. It acknowledges them, sees them, and creates real full space for them. It’s timeless. If the world don’t got me, I know Solange and this album do.”*

> “To me, the ‘it’ Solange sings of ridding herself of is the melancholy and self-doubt that lives in all of us. I think it’s a song all women, but especially Black women, can see themselves in” – Isabel

## STEPH, 23

*““Cranes In The Sky” is a tale as old as time for any woman who fills an emotional void with productivity and being proactive. I’ve never felt so seen in a song, and I’m always reminded to face my emotions before I’m faced with emotions.”*

## CHENAI, 17

*“I remember listening to ASATT for the first time and feeling a sense of recognition that I had never experienced with music in quite the same way. It gave me a different perspective on being a Black girl and made me think about parts of myself that I had not always known how to understand.*

*“As I get older, I hear it differently. Certain songs take on a different meaning depending on what I am going through. It has always felt like a love letter from a Black woman to other Black women, but I also hear it like notes from a big sister. Sometimes I hear a song and realise that she had already found the words for something I am only now beginning to understand. That is one of the most powerful things music can do. I* *t also makes space for the beauty of what Black people have created and continue to create. I think it is poetic that it has gone on to become an example of that itself. Ten years later, it has become part of the work that people can look to when thinking about the ways Black culture has shaped the world around us. It gives so many of us a place to recognise parts of ourselves and our own experiences, while also making room for the beauty within them. I am grateful that Solange made something that I can continue to grow alongside.”*

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S0qrinhNnOM)

## ISABEL, 24

*“It’s hard to think of a song I nod my head to in agreement more than “Cranes in the Sky.” On each listen, I find myself resonating with a different lyric, whether I’m drinking, dancing or working it away in that particular era of my life. To me, the ‘it’ Solange sings of ridding herself of is the melancholy and self-doubt that lives in all of us. I think it’s a song all women, but especially Black women, can see themselves in – life requires so much of us, and sometimes all you can do is keep trying, trying, trying to see what sticks.”*

## LYDIA, 27

*“* *When the album came out, ASATT felt like a new way of being seen as a woman of colour was being put in the mainstream. Seeing a Black woman discuss complex issues with her gentle voice, demanding understanding over beautiful beats and expressing anger in a way I hadn’t heard before. It was laced with history and emotion, being celebrated for being soft and vulnerable. I genuinely also do believe that “Don’t Touch My Hair” felt like the first time people understood the nuance of why this request was layered with deeper meaning, and I do believe it marked a shift in the end of being pet by strangers, so thank you for that too, Solange.”*

## LIV, 25

*“* *The album is a sanctuary. The validation of Black interiority, of Black grief and self-determination without being positioned as something that needs translation. It’s gentle while also being poignant, and that gentleness allows the themes to endure. The themes will always resonate because they are part of \[our\] experience, the cultural, historical, the personal, the political. But the format she delivered it in was actually really about softness with a certain respect for vulnerability that acted as a space-making moment. It’s restorative.”*

> “Sometimes I hear a song and realise that she had already found the words for something I am only now beginning to understand. That is one of the most powerful things music can do” – Chenai

## ZAINAB, 25

*“To me, ASTT is important because it’s by and for Black women – both explicitly (“Don’t Touch My Hair) and implicitly (“Rise”). And I know its impact on me only grows over time because it feels like regardless of our performance in the classroom or the workplace, the threshold for proper protections, fair compensation and recognition just keeps getting farther and farther away.*

*“It’s habitual for me after an especially hard day or week to put on my headphones and sing with Solange, “i tried to change it with my hair, i ran my credit card bill up … but that just made me even sadder” – as if no one else in the world could possibly understand, because that’s exactly how it feels.”*

## NOOR, 23

*“As an artist trying to navigate my way through predominantly white spaces, I spent a lot of my adolescence internalising daily microaggressions and categorisations that ultimately led to me making myself smaller, more digestible and palatable. Solange’s album is where I go when I need a reminder that I do not need to bow to the institutions and people who do not get me. Black women are not a monolith; we exist in every form. ASATT is the anchor I lean on when I find myself subconsciously moulding myself into the shapes people expect me to be.”*

## CARA, 26

*"ASATT was an album that introduced a sometimes misguided and wandering generation of young Black creative minds to themselves. It was for many of us the first time the Black diasporan experience was being articulated in the pop zeitgeist, and it felt like an open-armed homecoming. It had all the reasoning and the deep thought, but also in a very Black way, it also had the warmth, the forgiveness and understanding for those of us who had either lost our way home, or who hadn’t even yet started on our journeys.*

*“Being Black can be a surrealist experience in itself: you’re constantly pointing out things in a world that is adamant in pulling the cover over your eyes, convincing you that it wasn’t really racist, it wasn’t really misogynoir. It’s like stumbling through a dark labyrinth, grasping at the air for things you know to be at odds with your entire existence – but as soon as you’ve caught it, they’ve somehow managed to change and reconfigure the object.* *These topics that Solange lyricised are conversations that Black people had long been having behind closed doors with family, friends or romantic partners – yet hearing them on an album of this scale was truly liberating. Because of this, ASATT remains one of the defining albums of the modern Black diasporan experience, continuing to resonate today because its core mission endures: For Us, By Us."*
